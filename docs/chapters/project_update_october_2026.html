Las monjas, Isla Isabel, Nayarit. Photo credit: Miguel Betancourt-Lozano. Flickr.

This page presents the most updated current status scores for the Gulf of California Ocean Health Index, reflecting changes recommended by the Expert and Goalkeeper Working Groups. To compare how regions are doing over time, hover over each plot; a score of 100 means a goal’s condition is equal to its reference point.

Tourism & Recreation

The Tourism & Recreation goal measures the quantity, quality, and sustainability of experiences that people have when visiting coastal and marine areas. This goal emphasizes the personal and cultural value of coastal tourism and recreational activities as opposed to the revenue generated by them.

We evaluated three dimensions of tourism and recreational use: quantity, quality, and sustainability. All indicators are measured for coastal municipalities within 50 km of the Gulf shoreline, integrating the best available open-source data on international and domestic visitation, recreation opportunities, and sustainability practices.

🚢 Cruises & ANP Personnel Density (exploratory; not in TR score)

Cruise activity should be in Tourism & Recreation, but it is not scored in the October 2026 overall TR score and remains NA. The directionality of the reference point is not obvious: México has not said cruise tourism should increase or decrease, and while cruise lines support local tourism economies, many locals want it to decrease. A 7- to 10-hour port stop is also a different timeframe from a typical overnight tourist visit, and passengers are not split by domestic versus international visitors, so it does not fit well into the Quantity component.

That said, as it was highlighted by experts as important to explore, we investigated using the average 2016–2019 baseline as the maximum cruise pressure. Therefore, if in a given year and region there were more passengers than that mean, the current status score would lower. We encountered mixed opinions regarding cruises, and it became clear that a future working group should define what sustainability looks like for Gulf cruises before we incorporate this into the Index.

It should still be emphasized as a potential problem/area of need, especially in the Western Southern Gulf, which had a high rate of increase of passengers per port post-COVID. Additionally, intensified cruise tourism has also been linked to biological effects on whale shark habitat (Mongabay, May 2025).

Protected area personnel density (explored; not in the Tourism score)

We also explored protected area personnel density as a possible Sustainability investment indicator: how close each Área Natural Protegida (ANP) is to the staffing levels that Appleton et al. (2022) associate with effective management (Nature Sustainability).

Fiscal investment (budgets and returned fees) and SIMEC management-effectiveness ratings tell us about money and assessed capacity, but not whether protected areas actually have enough people on the ground. Global evidence suggests many protected areas are understaffed relative to what effective management requires. We therefore tested a simple density score (people per square kilometer) against published reference densities, as a candidate complement to the scored investment layer. It remains exploratory because the transparency extract does not yet cover every Gulf protected area (notably Baja California and Baja California Sur staffing were not provided within PNT Request 340008300059326).

How we scored it, and recommend incorporating it when data is available for all protected areas in the GOC.

  1. Read annual personnel counts by protected area (2018–2025) from a CONANP response on the Plataforma Nacional de Transparencia (SAI 340008300059326).
  2. Join each protected area to its surface area (hectares) from CONANP’s national ANP list, convert to km², and compute people per km². We use total surface (terrestrial + marine) so marine protected areas are not understated.
  3. Restrict to coastal, marine, island, or otherwise Gulf-linked protected areas (for example Alto Golfo, Islas del Golfo, Isla San Pedro Mártir, beach sanctuaries, Isla Isabel and its marine zone, Islas Marietas, Islas Marías, Meseta de Cacaxtla, and El Pinacate). Inland sierras (Bavispe; Sierra de Álamos–Río Cuchujaqui) were dropped. Where the transparency table split Islas del Golfo into multiple rows, we summed staff before dividing by area.
  4. Compare density to two Appleton et al. references: all personnel for effective management = 1 person per 13.33 km²; rangers = 1 ranger per 25.90 km².
  5. Score = ((1,  / ) ). At or above the reference gets 100. Protected areas with zero staff in a year score 0 (not NA).

For the ranger-proxy plot we use CONANP’s operativo staff categories (permanent + eventual field staff), the closest field-staff proxy in this response. The all-staff plot uses total personnel.

What entered the scored Tourism goal instead: SIMEC i-efectividad C2 Administrativo y financiero and C3 Usos y beneficios, together with federal CONANP budgets (V003 and V005) and returned cobro de derechos from the SHCP. Protected area personnel density itself is not in the scored Tourism goal.

📚 Data Sources & Methods

This goal uses several data sources across Quantity, Quality, and Sustainability:

  • Quantity: SECTUR DataTur hotel monitoring reports for domestic and international arrivals. Where a municipality is missing from DataTur, we fill the gap with INEGI DENUE hotel employee models.
  • Quality: SESNSP municipal crime statistics, INEGI municipal population (crime per person), and INEGI DENUE dining, cultural, and recreation businesses within 50 km of the coast.
  • Sustainability: hotel certifications (Distintivo S, Green Key, Green Globe, Earthcheck, Preferred by Nature, and Calidad Ambiental Turística / PNAA: Certificados Expedidos 2025); FEE Blue Flag beaches with INEGI coastline and beach linework; CONANP public investment plus SHCP / NOSSA fee returns; and INEGI Censos Económicos for tourism job quality. Earthcheck, Preferred by Nature, and PNAA certifications are included in the scored hotel-certification component. Tourism management investment combines CONANP budgets and returned fees with CONANP SIMEC i-efectividad components C2 Administrativo y financiero (budget sufficiency, personnel, equipment, management infrastructure, and cobro de derechos) and C3 Usos y beneficios (economic benefits, sustainable production/use, and visitor infrastructure). The regional SIMEC score is the mean of C2 and C3; analysis years 2019–2022 use the 2020 SIMEC report and 2023–2025 use the 2023 report.
  • Cruises and protected area personnel density (explored, not scored): SECTUR cruise port statistics (passengers and port calls), joined to OHI regions, with INEGI coastal municipal population for the per-person plots. Protected area personnel density compares CONANP transparency staffing (people per km², using total ANP surface area) to Appleton et al. (2022) references of 1 person per 13.33 km² (all staff) and 1 ranger per 25.90 km² (operativo field staff); coastal/marine Gulf-linked protected areas only; still at the protected area level and incomplete for Baja California / Baja California Sur. Neither enters the scored Tourism goal; see the exploratory callout above. Cruises remain NA pending a future working group on cruise sustainability metrics.

Reference points (score of 100 when…)

  • Quantity: Plan México 2030 arrival targets: domestic arrivals +9.8% above 2024; international arrivals +30% above 2024.
  • Quality: safety: zero weighted tourism-relevant crimes per coastal inhabitant.
  • Quality: recreation services: Gulf-wide 90th percentile of dining/culture/recreation employees per tourist arrival.
  • Sustainability: hotel certifications: all large coastal hotels (≥100 employees within 50 km of the coast) hold at least one sustainability certification.
  • Sustainability: Blue Flag: all highly visited beach length is Blue Flag certified.
  • Sustainability: tourism job quality: compensation at or above each region’s 2003–2018 mean; about 90% of tourism jobs formalized; women’s formalization rate at least equal to men’s.
  • Sustainability: tourism management investment: CONANP budgets (v003, v005) at or above 2012–2018 means, plus full return of collected fees, combined with strong SIMEC C2/C3 management-effectiveness scores.
  • Exploratory (not in score): cruise passenger density at or below the 2016–2019 regional mean; protected area staffing at Appleton densities above.

Food Provisioning

The Food Provisioning goal measures how much and how sustainably seafood is being produced throughout the region, drawing on seafood production from wild-catch fisheries and from mariculture.

Figure 8. Interactive graph depicting this goal’s current status over time per OHI region. The scores shown here are not finalized (version 1.0; September, 2026).

📊 Component scores

This goal has two components. Open the plots below to see wild catch and mariculture separately.

This is the wild catch fisheries component, which evaluates stock health from CONAPESCA landings (cleaned Mex-Fisheries data). Stock status comes first from published assessments (RAM), then Mexico’s national fisheries chart (Carta Nacional Pesquera), then a catch-based model (CMSY) when better options are unavailable. A score of 100 means stocks are at their reference status.

Figure 9. Interactive graph depicting this goal’s current status over time per OHI region. The scores shown here are not finalized (version 1.0; June, 2026).

This is the Mariculture component, which evaluates aquaculture production (shrimp, bivalves, and finfish) reported to Mexico’s fisheries agency (CONAPESCA), with Monterey Bay Aquarium Seafood Watch as a sustainability coefficient.

Figure 10. Interactive graph depicting this goal’s current status over time per OHI region. The scores shown here are not finalized (version 1.0; July, 2026).

📚 Data Sources & Methods

Food Provisioning measures seafood that is sustainably produced by wild catch fisheries and mariculture. These plots still use version 1.0 scores.

  • Wild catch: CONAPESCA landings, using the cleaned Mex-Fisheries version from the University of Miami. Stock status comes first from published assessments (RAM), then from Mexico’s national fisheries chart (Carta Nacional Pesquera), then from a catch-based model (CMSY) when we have no better option. We also use catch per unit effort from CONAPESCA permit holders.
  • Mariculture: CONAPESCA aquaculture production (shrimp, bivalves, and finfish), with Monterey Bay Aquarium Seafood Watch scores as a sustainability coefficient. The production target follows the Programa Nacional de Pesca y Acuacultura (20% growth over five years).
  • Fishing records are assigned to OHI regions using geospatial keys from Cavieses-Nuñez, Morzaria-Luna, and colleagues (2025).

Livelihoods & Economies

The Livelihoods & Economies goal measures the jobs and revenue generated by marine-related industries through two parts: Livelihoods, which captures the quantity and quality of marine-related jobs in the Gulf, and Economies, which captures the revenue generated by those same sectors. The sectors we included as “marine-related” were: fishing, mariculture, seafood processing and trade, port and dock construction / management, boat building, tidal energy, oil and gas maritime transport, cargo maritime transport, marine tourist transport, coastal recreation, travel planners, accommodations and food / drink, and environmental research / consulting.

This is the Economies component (formal marine-related revenue). Informal work is not added here, as we did not want to overstate revenue. Region 2 is NA.

📚 Data Sources & Methods

Livelihoods & Economies measures jobs and revenue from marine-related industries. Livelihoods captures the quantity and quality of marine-related jobs. Economies captures revenue from marine-related sectors.

  • Jobs and revenue: INEGI Censos Económicos (2003, 2008, 2013, 2018, 2023) for marine-related businesses. INEGI population is used so we can talk about jobs per coastal resident, and to adjust regions that span both the Gulf and Pacific coasts. Region 2 is NA because municipal census data are not enough to isolate Gulf-side activity.
  • Informality (current practice): INEGI ENOE builds a yearly state–sector multiplier applied to Livelihoods (jobs and job quality) so informal marine-related workers are included. Economies (revenue) remains formal Economic Census revenue only.
  • Reference points (score of 100 when…)
    • Livelihoods: number of jobs: each region’s own 2019 marine-related jobs per coastal resident (within-region; at or above → 100).
    • Livelihoods: quality of jobs: Gulf-wide 90th percentile of employer social-security / benefits per job across regions and census years.
    • Economies: revenue: each region’s own 2019 marine-related operating revenue (within-region; at or above → 100).
    • Because jobs and revenue use within-region references, a small region can score high relative to itself even if absolute activity is modest.

Sense of Place

The Sense of Place goal (in Spanish, Sentido de Pertenencia) measures how well we are maintaining the protection of coastal and marine species and places that provide a sense of shared memory, heritage, identity, and belonging for Gulf residents. It is built from three parts: Iconic Species, Lasting Special Places, and Coastal Community Engagement. These represent the mean risk status of culturally iconic species, the management condition of “special places,” and whether lasting infrastructure exists to support coastal communities in caring for and engaging with the Gulf. In practice, however, this goal currently measures only Iconic Species and Lasting Special Places.

This score does not measure if communities have a sense of place, nor whether iconic species or special places provide a sense of place. Rather, it measures whether or not there is consistent and effective management to help preserve these special species and places. In an ideal world, this goal would also measure whether there exist local organizations and institutions to help steward a sense of place for generations to come.

Figure 15. Interactive graph depicting this goal’s current status over time per OHI region. The scores shown here are version 1.1 (October, 2026).

📊 Component scores

We calculated Iconic Species sub-scores per region by evaluating the average risk status of species deemed “iconic”. We provide a detailed explanation of exactly how we did this in SI Section S-SP. Summarily, we evaluated species as iconic if they met at least one of the following criteria: (1) they have local cultural meaning beyond being regularly caught and eaten as seafood; (2) they are dependent on the health of the Gulf, as a marine species or obligate coastal species; (3) they are important to the general coastal community, not just biologists or conservationists. We evaluated their risk status according to risk status indicators published by (in order of priority) the Mexican federal government, the International Union for Conservation of Nature, or the international biodiversity trade law. See the most recent iconic species list.

Figure 16. Interactive graph depicting this goal’s current status over time per OHI region. The scores shown here are version 1.1 (October, 2026).

We calculated Lasting Special Places sub-scores per region per year by averaging the management effectiveness scores under various legal protections for five categories of “special places” that we identified through extended discussion. Summarily, we generated a list of special places and bound them into the following categories: (1) special protected areas and special habitats, (2) popular dive sites, (3) fishing spots, (4) archaeological sites, and (5) other. We then evaluated whether they fell within an area that had codified regulatory protections (versus unmanaged) and estimated whether that protection was effective or not. Effectiveness of each protection was determined using Comisión Nacional de Áreas Naturales Protegidas (CONANP)’s i-efectividad scores, available on the Sistema de Información, Monitoreo y Evaluación para la Conservación (SIMEC) platform. Management effectiveness of other types of protection was quantified using expert opinion.

Figure 17. Interactive graph depicting this component’s current status over time per OHI region. The scores shown here are version 1.1 (October, 2026).

Finally, we attempted to evaluate Coastal Community Engagement by measuring the civic, educational, and social means as an important component of maintaining and preserving the connection people have to the ocean. Initially we explored integrating beach cleanup programs and whether non-natural special places are sustained over time, such as museums and cultural centers, but it became clear that the proxies based upon the best available data are too narrow and not actually representative of sustained community engagement across the Gulf. Ultimately, we chose to exclude these scores from the final Sense of Place calculation by setting them equal to NA. We identified how we would like to achieve this in future iterations of the index here: Próximos pasos para la participación de las comunidades costeras. For now, the subgoal will remain NA until it can be properly assessed.

📚 Data Sources & Methods

Sense of Place measures how well we are sustainably caring for the marine and coastal places and species that provide heritage, shared memory, and identity for Gulf communities.

The October 2026 overall score is the mean of Iconic Species and Lasting Special Places only. Coastal Community Engagement is NA until the civic/educational/social engagement framework has data.

  • Iconic Species: Expert-final Keep list after expert review (most recent iconic species list); occurrence from Morzaria-Luna et al. (2027); risk status from NOM-059, IUCN Red List, and CITES. After many conversations in goalkeeper groups and follow-up emails about commercially caught species and whether they should be considered iconic, the group updated the definition. Final criteria: iconic species must (1) have local cultural meaning (e.g., spiritual, religious, heritage, or deep aesthetic meaning) beyond being regularly caught and eaten as seafood; (2) be dependent on the health of the Gulf (a marine species or a coastal obligate); and (3) be important to the general coastal community, not just biologists or conservationists. The group also reconsidered “existence value” as a criterion and decided against it: every species has existence value, and that alone does not justify an “iconic” designation. Applying the criteria consistently, several charismatic megafauna that are valued for tourism or conservation but not clearly celebrated as local cultural icons were excluded, while species such as gray whales (Eschrichtius robustus), humpbacks (Megaptera novaeangliae), California sea lions (Zalophus californianus), bottlenose and common dolphins (Tursiops truncatus, Delphinus delphis, Delphinus capensis), whale sharks (Rhincodon typus), and mantas (Mobula spp.) were retained. For shrimp, only Pacific blue shrimp (Penaeus stylirostris) was kept for its stronger ties to regional identity; white, brown, and pink shrimp (Penaeus vannamei, Penaeus californiensis, and Penaeus brevirostris) were removed as primarily commercial/culinary species under criterion 1.
  • Lasting Special Places: experts identified GoC special places (protected areas, dive sites, fishing spots, archaeology, habitats, and other places). The management effectivness condition of these special places comes from CONANP SIMEC i-efectividad, Ramsar management plans, and protected area polygons (WDPA, CONANP, HEM). Priority dive sites come from Arcos-Aguilar et al. (2021) and CBMC GCMP layers. Archaeology sites come from INAH.
  • Coastal Community Engagement (pre-EWG plots shown for comparison; not in overall): SEMARNAT Ama tu Playa beach adoptions (adoptaunaplaya.com) and INEGI DENUE sector 71 cultural establishments within 10 km of the coast. Rework notes: CCE Finalizing Methods and continued.

Reference points (score of 100 when…)

  • Iconic Species: mean conservation-risk status of iconic species in the region is at the lowest-risk level (species not listed as at risk under NOM-059 → IUCN → CITES priority).
  • Lasting Special Places: expert-listed special places are under strong legal protection with high management effectiveness (e.g. SIMEC i-efectividad near 100, or equivalent category tops for other protection types).
  • Coastal Community Engagement: NA in the October 2026 overall (not scored).

Access Opportunities

The Access Opportunities goal is intended to measure how much access members of local communities have to coastal spaces and marine resources that allow them to thrive. It is built from two parts: Artisanal Fishing Opportunities, which represents the “biological access” fishers have to artisanally fished stocks, and Local Coastal Access, which represents the “physical access” all people have to the beach. In practice, this goal currently measures only Artisanal Fishing Opportunities.

Figure 20. Interactive graph depicting this goal’s current status over time per OHI region. The scores shown here are not finalized (version 1.0; September, 2026).

📊 Component scores

This goal has two components: Artisanal Fishing Opportunities and Local Coastal Access. The overall score uses Artisanal Fishing Opportunities only; Local Coastal Access is NA in this version (see below).

This is the Artisanal Fishing Opportunities component: the artisanal share of CONAPESCA landings, with stock status from published assessments (RAM), then Mexico’s national fisheries chart (Carta Nacional Pesquera), then a catch-based model (CMSY) when needed.

Figure 21. Artisanal Fishing Opportunities current status by OHI region. The scores shown here are not finalized (version 1.0; August, 2026).

This is the Local Coastal Access component. Local Coastal Access is NA for this first version. To calculate these sub-scores we worked to estimate the legal protection of, management of, and realized access to local coastal beaches. We were able to estimate legal protection and management of local beaches, but were unable to reliably estimate realized access. Ultimately, we chose to exclude these scores from the final Access Opportunities calculation by setting them equal to NA because we felt that, without some estimate of realized access, they were misleading. The diagram below shows those three pieces. Methods for realized access were developed and tested for La Paz (see Coastal Access: La Paz), but that process showed the effort was beyond the scope of this project. Good results also need local expert review, which we did not have, and that led to issues such as mislabeled beaches that we could not easily fix. Efforts to map local beaches are underway in several places, but those maps are not available yet. Our hope is that they will eventually become the basis for scores for this metric.

Figure 22. Conceptual diagram of Local Coastal Access (legal protection, management and enforcement, and realized access). Local Coastal Access is held out as NA in this version of the Index.

📚 Data Sources & Methods

Access Opportunities (formerly Artisanal Opportunities) measures whether members of coastal communities, including small-scale fishing communities and pueblos originarios, have access to marine resources that allow them to thrive. These plots still use version 1.0 scores.

  • Artisanal Fishing Opportunities: CONAPESCA landings (cleaned Mex-Fisheries data), keeping the artisanal share of catch. Stock status uses the same hierarchy as Food Provisioning: published assessments (RAM), then Mexico’s national fisheries chart (Carta Nacional Pesquera), then a catch-based model (CMSY). Records are assigned to OHI regions with the Cavieses-Nuñez and Morzaria-Luna geospatial keys.
  • Local Coastal Access: NA (completed decision). We looked at legal protection under ZOFEMAT, municipal ZOFEMAT offices, local beach inventories, and national PROFEPA enforcement capacity. Realized-access methods are described on the Coastal Access: La Paz page but are not in this version, so Local Coastal Access stays NA so true coastal access is not misrepresented. Read more in the executive summary.

Habitat Services

The Habitat Services goal measures the contemporary versus historic capacity of coastal habitats to buffer against storms, flooding, and erosion; control, filter, and mitigate anthropogenic pollutants; and mitigate global climate change by sequestering carbon locally.

We estimated Habitat Services using three parts—coastal protection, pollution filtration, and carbon sequestration—among four of the nine biome types described under Biodiversity: mangrove forests, non-mangrove coastal wetlands, seagrass meadows, and coral reefs. In an ideal scenario, we would also include soft-bottom habitats and the open ocean, since these biomes also contribute to pollution control and carbon sequestration; however, we did not have the data necessary to estimate their contributions relative to a historical baseline.

Biodiversity

The Biodiversity goal is intended to quantify the current status of key “biomes” as proxies for species diversity, abundance, and ecosystem function. We use the term “biome” broadly to refer to either biogenic habitats, where living organisms support foundational ecological processes (e.g., coral reefs), or geophysical habitats, where ecological communities are supported by non-living features (e.g. the pelagic open ocean). In reality, these biome types are highly interconnected and intermixed, with living organisms and non-living resources that move across boundaries. In practice, however, it was useful for us to treat these biomes as distinct typologies so that we could evaluate their current status using habitat-based indicators.

We evaluated nine biome types under Biodiversity: mangrove forests, non-mangrove coastal wetlands and scrubland, seagrass meadows, coral reefs, rocky reefs, soft-bottom habitats of the coastal shelf, deep soft-bottom habitats, rhodolith beds, and the shallow pelagic open ocean.

Clean Waters

The Clean Waters goal measures the contamination risk of key pollutants (or technically the lack thereof) as a proxy for coastal water quality and the degree to which coastal waters remain unpolluted by human sources. In the Gulf, those sources include but are not limited to oil spills, synthetic chemical dumping and runoff, nutrient loading (eutrophication), disease pathogens, and floating trash. People are sensitive to these types of contamination, especially in areas used for recreation, and place a high existence value on simply knowing that clean waters exist.

We evaluated four pollutant types under Clean Waters: eutrophication, chemicals, pathogens, and marine plastics, all of which have been shown to impact human and non-human communities throughout the Gulf.

Figure 29. Interactive graph depicting this goal’s current status over time per OHI region. The scores shown here are version 1.1 (October, 2026).

📊 Component scores

Eutrophication measures excess nitrogen reaching the coast from wastewater, agriculture, livestock, and shrimp aquaculture.

We kept the source equations and coefficients from Romero-Gil et al. (2026) Supplemental Table 6, and adapted the steps by allocating municipal loads to Gulf watersheds. Agriculture was located with CONABIO USV cropland; livestock with FAO GLW4 density rasters; and shrimp farms with USV pond area. That lets us allocate total nitrogen to each coastal watershed, then use pourpoints associated with each watershed to identify where nitrogen would likely flow into the Gulf. To compare across regions, we divide total nitrogen for each region and year by the coastline (km) of the associated OHI region.

For scoring, we compare each observation to the average of the top five coastline-standardized nitrogen values across all regions and years. The ideal is zero nitrogen. We then linearly rescale pressure so that an observation at that top-five mean reference receives a pressure of 0.5 and a status of 50, and status ranges from 50 to 100. Experts preferred this approach because nutrient loading is not as automatically harmful as chemical pollution, the Gulf does not show a clear widespread eutrophication signal (for example, nearshore chlorophyll-a has tracked offshore concentrations over time), and a linear rescale keeps the same trends and regional rankings. A high nitrogen load per kilometer near the historical maximum gets a lower score, and a low load gets a higher score. These scores now vary across years, including Nayarit and northern Jalisco in the Southeastern Gulf region.

Figure 30. Interactive graph depicting this goal’s current status over time per OHI region. The scores shown here are version 1.1 (October, 2026).

Pathogens measures Enterococcus levels at monitored beaches (Playas Limpias). A score of 100 means waters are at the ideal of 0 MPN/100mL; scores fall toward 0 as concentrations approach Mexico’s beach suitability ceiling of 200 MPN/100mL.

📚 Data Sources & Methods

This goal uses 4 components. Chemicals is itself the average of five layers:

  • Eutrophication / nutrient loading: anthropogenic total nitrogen (wastewater, agriculture, livestock, shrimp aquaculture), reproducing Romero-Gil et al. 2026 with equations and coefficients from Supplemental Table 6. Loads are allocated to Gulf watersheds (CONABIO USV cropland; FAO GLW4 livestock; USV shrimp pond area), routed to the coast with watershed pourpoints, and standardized as tonnes N per year per km of OHI-region coastline. Status compares each observation to the mean of the top five nitrogen-per-kilometer values across regions and years; at that reference, pressure is rescaled to 0.5 and status to 50, so status ranges from 50 to 100. Scores vary across years, including Nayarit and northern Jalisco.
    • What changed from the earlier snapshot: the previous Clean Waters page used a single-year Romero-Gil et al. 2026 eutrophication snapshot held constant over time (and Region 9 was unscored). The current scores replace that with time-varying nitrogen status, including Nayarit and northern Jalisco.
  • Pathogens: CONAGUA Playas Limpias Enterococcus monitoring (2018–2025; no 2020 sampling).
  • Chemicals (equal-weight average of five subcomponents; microplastics are not yet included):
    • Pesticides (kept for this round): CONABIO USV agricultural land-use polygons and FAOSTAT national pesticide application rates (kg/ha), after conferring with Miguel Betancourt Lozano (September 2026). Crop-specific rates (SIAP crop types + COFEPRIS/SENASICA labels, calibrated to FAOSTAT) are deferred to a future iteration.
    • Pharmaceuticals: CONAGUA SINA wastewater-treatment plant effluent; FAO livestock density (GLW4) with SIAP slaughter tonnes and published antimicrobial-use rates; INEGI USV shrimp-aquaculture area.
    • Hazardous waste: SEMARNAT and ASEA inventories of contaminated and remediated sites within 50 km of the coast.
    • Untreated wastewater: INEGI CNGMD untreated municipal discharge points.
    • Vessel hydrocarbons: VMS fishing-vessel effort (hours) as a proxy for hydrocarbon risk.
  • Plastics: VMS fishing data from Juan Carlos Villaseñor (Mexico’s SISMEP), paired with Gilman et al. (2021) rates for abandoned, lost, or discarded gear (ghost gear); megatrash is not yet scored. Scores are already current status.

Reference points (score of 100 when…)

  • Eutrophication: ideal = zero excess nitrogen per km of coastline. Status is scaled so the mean of the top five Gulf TN/km observations scores 50 (and status ranges from 50 to 100), rather than forcing that high load to zero like Chemicals.
  • Pathogens: ideal = 0 MPN/100mL Enterococcus; score approaches 0 as concentrations reach Mexico’s beach suitability ceiling of 200 MPN/100mL.
  • Chemicals: ideal = no pesticide, pharmaceutical, hazardous-waste, untreated-wastewater, or vessel-hydrocarbon pressure; high pressure is usually set by the mean of the top five Gulf region–years (untreated wastewater uses the maximum observed density).
  • Plastics (ghost gear): ideal = no abandoned-fishing-gear risk; high risk set by the mean of the top five regional ghost-gear risk values.

Natural Products

The Natural Products goal is intended to measure the amount, benefits, and sustainability of legally harvested marine resources produced for cultural or commercial purposes but not used for food or human consumption. Natural products include essentially any goods derived from living organisms, from pearls and seashells to fertilizers and bio-pharmaceuticals, that can be sold as secondary market products.

We evaluated four natural product types under this goal: aquarium trade species, fish used for fish oil and fish meal, fish and shrimp residues, and coastal mineral production of salt and gypsum.

📚 Data Sources & Methods

Natural Products measures the amount, benefits, and sustainability of legally harvested marine resources produced in the Gulf of California for cultural or commercial purposes that are not used for food. The October 2026 version 1.1 score averages the sectors that clear an inclusion filter in each region.

Sectors scored in version 1.1

  1. Fish oil and fish meal (FOFM): CONAPESCA (Mex-Fisheries clean version).
  2. Ornamental trade species (ORNA): same analysis as the former AQRM sector, renamed. Score is annual production relative to mean regional production from 2018–2022, with a linear penalty for over- or under-harvest (50% over or under yields a score of 50).
  3. Fisheries residues (RESD): demersal fish residues only (shrimp residues dropped; almost no evidence of shrimp-residue sales). Reference assumes full utilization of harvest: about 30% of fish biomass edible meat and 30% saleable residues (FAO-supported).
  4. Coastal mining (MINE): El Anuario Estadístico de la Minería Mexicana for Sal (updated through 2024) and Yeso/gypsum (2018–2024). MINE is the average of salt and gypsum sub-scores. Salt reference: mines should maintain production similar to Hermosillo (mean 2018–2024), capped at 1, then averaged across mines in the region. Gypsum reference: mean regional production 2018–2022, with a linear penalty for over- or under-harvest (non-renewable open-pit mining).

Dropped after EWG 3.3: SHEL (poor shell-residue data) and FINS (little evidence of shark finning; dropping FINS lowered many regional scores by a few points).

Inclusion-exclusion filter (all sectors except MINE): a sector is included in a region only if mean regional production from 2018–2024 is at or above the median production across all regions for that period; otherwise the sector score is NA. Regions with only negligible sector production therefore receive NA for those sectors (and may be NA overall).

Stock status for fishery-linked sectors still uses the same RAM, Carta Nacional Pesquera, and CMSY hierarchy as Food Provisioning where applicable. Scores were shared with the NP goalkeeper group for review; whether NP enters the final index average remains a core-team decision (NP tends to pull Regions 5–9 down relative to other goals).
